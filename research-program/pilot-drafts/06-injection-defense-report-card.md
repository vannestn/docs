# The Injection Defense Report Card: A Standardized, Independent Adaptive-Robustness Protocol and Grader-Robustness Audit for Prompt-Injection Defenses
*Target venue: an AI-security / ML-safety workshop (NeurIPS/ICLR safety track, USENIX/IEEE-S&P-adjacent workshop) or arXiv preprint · Draft — Introduction & Methods only*

## Abstract

Prompt injection is the leading security risk for tool-using language-model agents, and a growing catalogue of defenses report near-zero attack success rate (ASR) against fixed test suites. Recent work shows this reported robustness collapses under *adaptive* attack: when an attacker is allowed to optimize against a published defense, most defenses fall to ASR above 90%. The field consensus is therefore that static evaluation systematically overstates robustness. Yet the public infrastructure has not caught up: the most-cited agent leaderboard evaluates defenses against a fixed attack set, and there is no standardized, independent, *recurring* protocol that grades a defense's stated robustness against what survives adaptation under a documented, fixed attack budget. We do not claim to invent adaptive attacks. Our contribution is a standardized graded protocol: (i) a fixed, fully-specified adaptive attack budget wrapped around released attack code; (ii) an A–F "Adaptive Robustness Grade" keyed to the *overclaim gap* between a defense's reported static ASR and its measured adaptive ASR; and (iii) OracleProof, a cross-oracle gameability audit of the success judge itself (LLM-judge vs. multi-judge consensus vs. programmatic state-diff), since a gameable grader would invalidate every number the report card reports. We pre-register success and kill criteria and release a v1 card grading roughly 10–15 defenses that advertise robustness. The protocol runs on a single rented GPU and a small open agent, within a USD 500 budget.

## 1. Introduction

Language-model agents that call tools over untrusted data are now deployed for email triage, web browsing, code execution, and financial workflows. This deployment surface exposes them to *prompt injection*: adversarial instructions embedded in tool outputs, documents, or web pages that hijack the agent into pursuing an attacker-chosen goal rather than the user's [8]. Prompt injection is widely regarded as the top security risk for tool-using agents, and the response has been a rapidly growing catalogue of defenses—input filters, detection classifiers, prompt-level isolation, fine-tuned "secure" backbones, and design-by-construction control/data-flow separation [3, 5]. A recurring feature of these proposals is a headline robustness number: many report that they stop close to 100% of injection attempts.

The central methodological problem is that these headline numbers are almost always measured against a *static* attack—a fixed list of injection strings, or attacks generated by an optimizer that was not tuned against the specific defense. Adversarial machine learning learned decades ago that this is the wrong evaluation: the attacker moves second and can adapt arbitrarily to a published defense, so a defense must be evaluated against an attacker who explicitly optimizes to defeat *its* design [7]. When that adaptive standard is applied to injection defenses, the reported robustness largely evaporates. Recent large-scale studies bypass diverse recent defenses with adaptive attack success rates above 90% for most [1], and adaptive attacks against indirect-injection defenses for agents break all evaluated defenses with ASR consistently above 50% [2]. The gap between reported static ASR and measured adaptive ASR is not a minor calibration error; for many defenses it is the difference between "essentially unbreakable" and "essentially broken."

Despite this now-settled scientific consensus, the *public evaluation infrastructure* has not been aligned to it, and this is the specific gap we address. Three shortfalls stand out. First, the most widely used public agent-injection benchmark, AgentDojo [8], is an excellent dynamic environment with a programmatic success oracle, but its live leaderboard is evaluated against a fixed attack set; defenses that post near-zero ASR there are being scored under exactly the static regime the adaptive literature has discredited. Second, adaptive attacks today are executed heterogeneously—each paper defines its own attack budget, optimizer, target model, and success oracle—so numbers are not comparable across defenses, and a buyer cannot tell whether a "B" reflects a robust defense or a weak attacker. Standardized *toolkits* for optimization-based evaluation have begun to appear [6], but they standardize the attack machinery, not a graded, recurring, independent verdict on the *claim*. Third, the community has been explicitly asked for exactly this missing layer: the adaptive-attack literature ends by calling for a recurring, community-maintained adaptive-evaluation regime rather than one-off audits [1]. No such standardized, dated, independently-run report card currently exists.

There is a further, less-obvious hazard that any such report card must confront: the success judge itself. Whether an injection "succeeded" must be decided by some oracle—an LLM-as-judge, a consensus of judges, or a programmatic state-diff over the agent's environment. Recent work shows that automated success oracles are themselves fragile and adversarially manipulable: benign-looking wrappers flip LLM-judges on jailbreak-success scoring between 57% and 100% of the time, and even a dedicated safety classifier can have 70% of its confident positives flipped under a small white-box optimization budget [4]. If the grader can be gamed, every ASR the report card publishes is untrustworthy in *both* directions—overclaiming and underclaiming robustness alike. An adaptive-robustness report card that does not audit its own oracle is therefore incomplete, and this grader-robustness slice is, to our knowledge, unaddressed for the agent-injection setting.

We stress what we do *not* claim. Adaptive attacks are established methodology and field consensus [1, 2, 7]; we neither invent them nor claim to attack harder than a well-resourced frontier lab. Our authority derives instead from standardization and neutrality: a *documented, fixed attack budget* makes grades comparable and makes the honest limitation ("a higher grade may mean the budget was too small") explicit rather than hidden, and an independent evaluator with no defense to sell has no incentive to launder a favorable number. The scientific contribution is not a new attack; it is a reproducible protocol that converts an established attack into a comparable, honest, recurring grade, plus an audit that certifies the grader is not the weakest link.

We make the following contributions:

1. **A standardized adaptive-evaluation protocol** that wraps released adaptive-attack code in a *fully specified, fixed attack budget* (optimizer, iteration/query caps, compute, target model, and threat model), so that the adaptive ASR measured for any defense is reproducible and comparable across defenses.
2. **The Adaptive Robustness Grade**, an A–F rubric keyed to the *overclaim gap*—the signed difference between a defense's self-reported static ASR and its measured adaptive ASR under the fixed budget—operationalizing robustness *honesty* as the primary graded quantity rather than raw ASR.
3. **OracleProof**, a cross-oracle gameability audit that adaptively pressures three success oracles (single LLM-judge, multi-judge consensus, programmatic state-diff) and reports a bidirectional gameability score, certifying whether the report card's own verdicts can be manipulated.
4. **A pre-registered, released v1 report card** grading roughly 10–15 injection defenses that advertise robustness, together with all code, seeds, budgets, and per-defense logs, plus explicit success and kill criteria and a stated re-run cadence.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Data and materials

**Evaluation substrate.** We build on AgentDojo [8], an openly released dynamic agent environment (MIT-licensed, public repository) comprising realistic tool-using tasks (e.g., an email/workspace client) with 97 user tasks and hundreds of security test cases. AgentDojo is chosen for three reasons: it exposes pluggable Agent / Attack / Defense interfaces, it ships a *programmatic* state-diff success oracle that does not depend on an LLM judge, and it is already the community's reference environment, so grades are interpretable relative to published numbers.

**Attack code.** We do not implement new attacks. We wrap released, peer-reviewed adaptive-attack implementations: the optimization-based toolkit OET [6] and the adaptive-attack recipes released with the indirect-injection study of Zhan et al. [2], both public. Where a defense's original paper ships its own adaptive appendix, we additionally reproduce that author-supplied attack as a lower bound.

**Defenses under test.** The sample is the set of publicly released injection defenses that (a) *advertise* a robustness number (a reported static ASR, break rate, or "provably secure" claim) and (b) provide code or a sufficiently specified procedure to reimplement within budget. This targets roughly 10–15 defenses spanning the main families: detection/classifier defenses (the AgentDojo detector baseline), prompt-isolation and spotlighting-style defenses, fine-tuned secure backbones, and design-by-construction defenses such as CaMeL [3]. Inclusion requires a locatable self-reported robustness claim, which becomes the static baseline in the overclaim gap; defenses without a locatable claim are excluded from the graded card and reported separately as "unclaimed."

**Sample construction.** For each defense we fix a task/injection sample of size \(n\) drawn from AgentDojo's security test cases, held identical across all defenses so that grades are comparable. Each item is a (user task, attacker goal, injection channel) triple with a deterministic success predicate defined by the environment state-diff oracle.

### 2.2 The core protocol: fixed-budget adaptive evaluation and the Adaptive Robustness Grade

We formalize the protocol as a fixed contract between a defense, an attacker, and a budget.

**Threat model and budget.** Fix a target agent model \(M\) and a defense \(D\). For each evaluation item \(x_i\), the attacker observes the full specification of \(D\) (its code, prompt, and any documented internals) and may optimize an injection \(a_i\) subject to a *fixed budget* \(B = (K, Q, C, T)\): \(K\) optimizer iterations, \(Q\) total model queries, \(C\) wall-clock/compute cap, and a fixed threat model \(T\) (which channels are attacker-controlled). \(B\) is declared *before* any defense is attacked and is identical across defenses. This is the operationalization of "the attacker moves second under a documented budget" [1, 7]: the budget is the load-bearing artifact that makes grades comparable and makes the "did we attack hard enough" caveat quantitative rather than rhetorical.

**Attack execution.** For each item we run a portfolio of released optimizers (search-based, gradient-based where weights are available, and author-supplied adaptive recipes), each capped by \(B\). Following the disjunction-over-portfolio scoring of the adaptive literature [1], item \(i\) is counted as broken if *any* optimizer in the portfolio drives the agent to satisfy the attacker goal within budget.

**Adaptive ASR.** Let \(o(\cdot)\) be the success oracle (Section 2.4) and \(s_i(D,B)=1\) iff item \(i\) is broken. The measured adaptive ASR of defense \(D\) is
\[
\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{adapt}}(D) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} s_i(D,B).
\]

**Overclaim gap.** Let \(\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{static}}(D)\) be the defense's *own reported* static attack success rate (or \(1-\text{reported robustness}\)), extracted verbatim from its paper/repo and recorded with a citation. The overclaim gap is
\[
\Delta(D) = \mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{adapt}}(D) - \mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{static}}(D).
\]
\(\Delta(D)\) is the primary graded quantity: it measures how far a defense's real adaptive robustness falls short of its advertised robustness. A defense that reports 2% static ASR but is broken 92% of the time has \(\Delta \approx 0.90\); a defense whose adaptive ASR matches its claim has \(\Delta \approx 0\).

**Adaptive Robustness Grade.** We map \((\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{adapt}}, \Delta)\) to a letter grade with a pre-registered, published rubric. The letter is driven jointly by *how robust* the defense actually is (low \(\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{adapt}}\)) and *how honest* the claim is (low \(\Delta\)):
- **A**: \(\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{adapt}} \le 0.10\) and \(\Delta \le 0.10\) (robust and honest);
- **B**: \(\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{adapt}} \le 0.30\) and \(\Delta \le 0.25\);
- **C**: \(\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{adapt}} \le 0.50\) or \(\Delta \le 0.40\);
- **D**: \(\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{adapt}} \le 0.75\);
- **F**: \(\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{adapt}} > 0.75\).
Grades are reported with the confidence interval on \(\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{adapt}}\) (Section 2.6); if the interval straddles a boundary the lower (more conservative-toward-the-defense) grade is assigned and the ambiguity is flagged. Exact thresholds are frozen in the pre-registration before any defense is run so the rubric cannot be tuned to produce a desired distribution of grades.

**Assumptions, stated explicitly.** (i) The fixed budget \(B\) is a lower bound on real-world attacker capability; a favorable grade certifies survival *at budget \(B\)*, not unconditional robustness. (ii) The self-reported \(\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{static}}\) is taken at face value from the defense's own publication; where a defense reports multiple numbers we use the most favorable one it advertises, which is the honest choice for an overclaim metric. (iii) Reimplementation fidelity is a risk; each reimplemented defense is validated against its paper's reported static ASR before adaptive attack, and defenses we cannot reproduce to within a tolerance are reported as "not reproduced" rather than graded.

### 2.3 Baselines and comparators

The protocol is compared against the two evaluation regimes it aims to replace, on the identical defense set and sample:
1. **Static evaluation (AgentDojo default).** The fixed attack set shipped with AgentDojo [8]. This reproduces the near-zero ASR that motivates the paper and quantifies the overclaim gap the card exists to expose.
2. **Non-adaptive optimized attack.** A generic optimizer run *without* conditioning on each defense's design (an off-the-shelf attack, not tuned to \(D\)), representing the "weak optimizer" regime the adaptive literature criticizes [1, 7].
3. **Author-supplied adaptive attack.** Where a defense ships its own adaptive appendix (e.g., CaMeL [3]), we reproduce it as a comparator, so the card distinguishes "we found a stronger attack" from "the author's own attack already broke the claim."
The contribution is not a single ASR but the *contrast* across these regimes under one fixed budget and one oracle.

### 2.4 Metrics

**Adaptive ASR and overclaim gap** are defined in Section 2.2. We additionally report:

**Break rate per attack family**, \(\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{adapt}}\) decomposed by optimizer type, to show which attack the disjunction credited.

**Oracle gameability (OracleProof).** Fix a defense–task pair and hold the underlying agent behavior fixed; construct adversarial perturbations of the agent transcript that do not change the true outcome (benign reframing that leaves any harmful action intact, per the wrapper attacks of [4]) and measure how often each oracle's verdict flips. For oracle \(o\), the bidirectional gameability score is
\[
G(o) = \max\big(\mathrm{FlipRate}_{\text{true}\to\text{false}}(o),\ \mathrm{FlipRate}_{\text{false}\to\text{true}}(o)\big),
\]
i.e., the larger of the false-safe and false-attacked flip rates. We compute \(G\) for the single LLM-judge, the \(k\)-of-\(m\) multi-judge consensus, and the programmatic state-diff oracle. For the deterministic state-diff oracle, which has no stochastic verdict to fool, we instead report a *spec-coverage* result: the fraction of documented environment side effects the predicate fails to capture, framed honestly as an incompleteness audit rather than an adaptive attack, following the caveat that a state-diff oracle is stressed by finding real actions that satisfy the goal while evading the predicate, not by optimizing a string against it.

**Oracle agreement.** Cohen's/Fleiss' \(\kappa\) between the three oracles on the graded sample, so readers can see whether the choice of oracle materially moves any grade.

**Reproduction fidelity.** \(|\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{static}}^{\text{reproduced}} - \mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{static}}^{\text{reported}}|\) per defense, gating whether a defense is graded.

A break "counts" only when the state-diff oracle confirms the attacker goal was achieved (for the graded card) so that the headline grades rest on the least-gameable oracle; the LLM-judge and consensus oracles are used for the OracleProof comparison and as a sensitivity check, never as the sole arbiter of a grade.

### 2.5 Human validation and labeling

Human labels are needed only to *calibrate and audit the oracle*, not to grade every item—the state-diff oracle is programmatic and requires no per-item human judgment. The author (one prior publication on LLM-as-judge for RAG hallucination detection) hand-labels a stratified sample of roughly 150–200 agent transcripts as attack-succeeded / failed / ambiguous. In one sentence, the labeler reads each transcript and decides whether the agent actually took the attacker's intended action. The expertise required is familiarity with the AgentDojo task semantics and prompt-injection success conditions—precisely the LLM-as-judge and eval methodology the author brings—so no external domain expert is required; this fits within the author's ~4–7 hours of available manual labeling. These labels serve two purposes: (i) validating the state-diff oracle and LLM-judge against ground truth (reporting precision/recall per the recommendation of [4]), and (ii) constructing the true-outcome anchor for the OracleProof flip-rate measurement. Because AgentDojo ships deterministic success predicates, the bulk of ground truth is *sourced from the existing environment* rather than newly annotated; human labeling is confined to the ambiguous slice and the oracle-audit sample, with at most a small paid second-annotator pass (~50 items) to estimate inter-annotator agreement on the ambiguous cases.

### 2.6 Statistical analysis

**Uncertainty on ASR.** Each \(\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{adapt}}(D)\) is a binomial proportion over \(n\) items; we report Wilson score 95% confidence intervals and, because items share a defense and model, cluster-bootstrap intervals (resampling tasks) as the primary interval. Grades are assigned from the point estimate but annotated with the interval; boundary-straddling intervals trigger the conservative-grade rule (Section 2.2).

**Overclaim gap.** \(\Delta(D)\) inherits the interval of \(\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{adapt}}\) (the reported static value is a fixed constant from the defense's paper). We test \(H_0: \Delta(D) \le 0\) against \(H_1: \Delta(D) > 0\) per defense (a one-sided proportion test that adaptive ASR exceeds the claimed static ASR), with Holm–Bonferroni correction across the ~10–15 defenses.

**Sample-size reasoning.** For a target margin of \(\pm 0.10\) on an ASR near 0.5 at 95% confidence, a Wilson interval requires \(n \approx 96\) items; AgentDojo's security test set comfortably exceeds this, so we fix \(n\) at the largest common subset runnable within budget across all defenses (targeting \(n \ge 100\) per defense). This powers detection of an overclaim gap of \(\ge 0.15\) with adequate confidence, which is far below the >0.90 gaps the adaptive literature reports [1, 2], so the study is over-powered for the effect it is most likely to see and appropriately powered for borderline defenses.

**Confound controls.** (i) The attack budget \(B\), target model \(M\), sample, and oracle are held identical across defenses, so cross-defense grade differences are attributable to the defense, not the harness. (ii) Reproduction fidelity gating (Section 2.4) prevents a mis-implemented defense from being graded. (iii) The non-adaptive-optimizer baseline (Section 2.3) isolates how much of any break is due to adaptivity rather than raw attack strength. (iv) OracleProof and inter-oracle \(\kappa\) control for the possibility that a grade is an artifact of oracle choice.

### 2.7 Pre-registered success and kill criteria

*This section is written as a pre-registration; thresholds are frozen before any defense is attacked.*

**Primary hypothesis.** Across the graded defenses, the median overclaim gap \(\Delta\) under the fixed budget \(B\) is substantially positive.

**Success (positive result).** We report a positive result if (a) for a majority of graded defenses the one-sided test rejects \(H_0:\Delta \le 0\) after Holm–Bonferroni correction, i.e., measured adaptive ASR significantly exceeds self-reported static ASR; **and** (b) OracleProof shows the state-diff oracle is *not* gameable beyond a pre-set tolerance (\(G(\text{state-diff-relevant spec coverage}) \) leaves headline grades unchanged when recomputed under the LLM-judge, i.e., no grade moves by more than one letter across oracles). Condition (b) is required: a positive robustness finding is only meaningful if the grader is certified.

**Kill / null (report and stop).** We report a null and stop if either: (a) after correction, fewer than half of graded defenses show a significant positive overclaim gap under budget \(B\)—i.e., under an honestly-documented feasible budget the solo protocol cannot demonstrate systematic overclaiming (the honest reading being "a ≤USD 500 budget is insufficient to referee these defenses," itself a publishable negative that quantifies the resource floor for independent auditing); **or** (b) OracleProof finds that all three oracles are gameable to the point that grades are not stable across oracles (\(>\) one letter of movement for a majority of defenses), in which case the report card cannot be trusted and we publish the grader-robustness failure as the primary finding instead of the card.

**Secondary/exploratory.** Per-family break rates and the non-adaptive vs. adaptive contrast are exploratory and reported without confirmatory claims.

### 2.8 Reproducibility, compute, and budget

**Models and versions.** The target agent \(M\) is a small open-weights instruction model (e.g., a 7B-class open model) so that gradient- and search-based attacks are feasible on modest hardware and results are fully reproducible without proprietary API access; the exact checkpoint hash is recorded. Oracle LLM-judges use pinned model versions with fixed decoding parameters, logged verbatim.

**Seeds and determinism.** All optimizers, sampling, and judge calls use fixed seeds; the sample subset, budget \(B\), and rubric thresholds are committed to the repository before execution. Each run emits a machine-readable log (per-item optimizer trace, oracle verdicts, and final grade).

**Compute and budget.** The evaluation is designed to run on a single rented GPU (a single high-memory accelerator suffices for a 7B-class target with released attack code) plus API calls for the LLM-judge oracle. The dominant cost is optimizer iterations across ~10–15 defenses at \(n\ge100\) items under a capped budget; the design is confirmed feasible within **USD 500** total (GPU rental plus judge API), consistent with the released-code, single-GPU footprint of the adaptive-evaluation toolkits we build on [6]. No IRB is required: no human subjects beyond the author's own labeling and one optional paid annotator on public, non-sensitive transcripts.

**Artifacts released.** We release: the fixed budget specification \(B\); the graded v1 report card with per-defense grades, \(\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{adapt}}\), \(\Delta\), and confidence intervals; the OracleProof gameability results and inter-oracle \(\kappa\); the harness wrapping the released attacks; all seeds and pinned model versions; and the human-labeled oracle-validation set. We commit to a stated re-run cadence so the card is a *recurring*, dated artifact rather than a one-off audit, directly answering the community call for a maintained adaptive-evaluation layer [1].
