# Sweep 02 — The Data Science of AI (curation, forensics, provenance, synthetic-data ecology, tokenizers)

*Research sweep, July 2026. Everything cited below comes from a page actually opened during this sweep; anything not directly verified is flagged ⚠️.*

**One-line thesis:** the study of *what models are trained on* — building open corpora, auditing what's inside them, tracing where data came from and whether anyone consented, measuring how model-generated text is recontaminating the web, and understanding how tokenization (how raw text is chopped into the sub-word units a model actually reads) warps everything downstream. It is one of the few AI subfields where landmark results are routinely produced by 2–8 person teams with little or no GPU budget.

> **How to read this brief.** Where the text names a paper or a technical term, a short plain-language gloss follows in parentheses or an em-dash — enough to tell what a paper *did/found* or what a concept *means* without opening the link.

## The shape of this space

Six sub-areas, in rough order of how much compute they need (least → most):

1. **Dataset documentation & retrospective auditing.** Writing the missing datasheet (a standardized "nutrition label" describing what's in a dataset and how it was made) for corpora already in wide use; quantifying duplicates, PII (personally identifiable information), toxicity, benchmark leakage (test questions accidentally present in training data), license status. Anchors: Datasheets-style retrospective audits ([Bandy & Vincent's BookCorpus datasheet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.05241) — after-the-fact documentation of a corpus nobody had ever inspected), corpus-analysis toolkits ([WIMBD, ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20707) — "What's In My Big Data?", tools to search/count what a giant corpus actually contains), and machine-readable metadata standards ([MLCommons Croissant](https://mlcommons.org/working-groups/data/croissant/), [paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.19546v3) — a common file format for describing datasets so tools can read them automatically). Mostly CPU + storage + patience.

2. **Data forensics & contamination auditing.** Detecting whether benchmarks or copyrighted text sit inside training sets, usually black-box (working only from model outputs, without access to the weights or training data). Anchors: [Proving Test Set Contamination in Black-Box LMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17623) (a statistical test based on *exchangeability* — if a model saw a benchmark in training it prefers the original question ordering over shuffled ones, which yields a provable p-value; ICLR 2024 oral); the [CONDA workshop](https://conda-workshop.github.io/) and its [community evidence database](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21530) (a crowd-maintained log of known contamination: 566 entries, 91 contaminated sources, 23 contributors); the [LM Contamination Index](https://github.com/hitz-zentroa/lm-contamination) (a manually curated database — literally a GitHub repo one person started). Counter-forensics exists too: [Evading Data Contamination Detection is (too) Easy](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.02823) (shows that lightly rephrasing benchmark items defeats current detectors).

3. **Data provenance, licensing & consent.** Tracing lineage/licenses of datasets (provenance = where each piece of data came from and under what terms) and measuring the collapse of the "data commons" (the shrinking pool of freely usable web text). Anchors: the [Data Provenance Initiative audit of 1,800+ datasets](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16787) (hand-traced the licenses/origins of popular training sets; published in [Nature Machine Intelligence](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-024-00878-8); found 70%+ license omission and 50%+ error rates on hosting sites); [Consent in Crisis](https://www.dataprovenance.org/Consent_in_Crisis.pdf) (a longitudinal audit — repeated over time — of robots.txt/ToS restrictions, the machine-readable rules and legal terms sites use to permit or forbid crawling: 28%+ of the most critical C4 sources fully restricted within a year); the ICML 2024 position paper [Data Authenticity, Consent & Provenance for AI are all broken](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12691) (argues the field has no working infrastructure for verifying where data came from or whether its use was permitted). Consent *infrastructure* is being built by [Spawning](https://haveibeentrained.com/) (a Do Not Train registry and ai.txt — an opt-out file, like robots.txt but for AI training).

4. **Open-data curation at scale.** Building and ablating (systematically adding/removing pieces to see what each contributes) pretraining corpora: [FineWeb](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.17557v1) (15T tokens from 96 Common Crawl snapshots plus the FineWeb-Edu classifier trick — a small model that scores web pages for educational quality so low-value text can be filtered out), [Dolma](https://allenai.org/blog/dolma-3-trillion-tokens-open-llm-corpus-9a0ff4b8da64) + its [open curation toolkit](https://github.com/allenai/dolma), [DataComp-LM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11794) (a *competition* format: everyone gets the same fixed raw data pool and competes on curation recipes — which filtering/dedup choices produce the best model), [Common Pile v0.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05209v1) (8TB of public-domain/openly-licensed text; two years of curation), [Common Corpus](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01732v1) (2T tokens, Pleias). The full corpus builds need clusters, but the *component science* (quality classifiers, dedup studies — measuring/removing duplicate text, license-detection pipelines, OCR-correction models — fixing errors from scanning old print) is small-scale.

5. **Synthetic-data ecology.** (How AI-generated text feeds back into the data supply.) Two threads: (a) *theory/empirics of model collapse* (the degradation that happens when models are trained on other models' output generation after generation) — the Nature 2024 Shumailov result ([*Nature* 631, d41586-024-02355-z](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-024-02355-z); the influential paper warning that recursive training on synthetic data destroys quality) vs. the rebuttal [Is Model Collapse Inevitable?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01413) showing that if synthetic data *accumulates alongside* real data rather than *replacing* it, test error stays bounded; a single-author theoretical note ([Borji, arXiv:2410.12954](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12954)) argues via KDE that the collapse "may be unavoidable" as a statistical-sampling phenomenon — i.e. it *supports*, not rebuts, Shumailov. And (b) *measurement of machine-generated text in the wild* — [A Shocking Amount of the Web is Machine Translated](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.103/) (finding: 57.1% of sentences in their multi-way-parallel web-corpus sample come from multi-way-parallel tuples — the same content appearing in 3+ languages — a fingerprint of low-quality machine translation, MT; primary-verified this pass), [The Rise of AI-Generated Content in Wikipedia](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08044) (finding: >5% of new English articles flagged as AI-written; primary-verified this pass), plus widely-cited industry estimates (Graphite: AI articles passed 50% of sampled new articles by May 2025 — ⚠️ built on the commercial SurferSEO detector; see [Futurism summary](https://futurism.com/artificial-intelligence/over-50-percent-internet-ai-slop)).

6. **Tokenizer effects.** Tokenization as a data artifact with downstream cost/fairness/safety consequences: 2–15× token-cost premiums for low-resource languages (a language whose script/words get chopped into many more tokens pays 2–15× more per sentence in both money and context budget — [Reducing Tokenization Premiums](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.13328), ["tokenizer tax" across European languages](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.24718)); glitch/under-trained tokens as a safety surface (rare tokens the model barely saw in training, which can trigger bizarre or unsafe behavior — [Fishing for Magikarp](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05417)). The field only got its first dedicated venue in 2025: [TokShop @ ICML 2025](https://icml.cc/virtual/2025/workshop/39998).

## Published exemplars (small-team bias)

1. **[Fishing for Magikarp: Automatically Detecting Under-trained Tokens](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05417)** — **2 authors** (Sander Land, Max Bartolo; Cohere), EMNLP 2024. Turned the folkloric "SolidGoldMagikarp" glitch into a general, automatic detection method using tokenizer analysis + weight indicators + prompting, run across many open models. Landed because it converted an internet curiosity into a reusable audit with safety implications, with [open code](https://github.com/cohere-ai/magikarp). *The archetype of solo-viable data forensics: no training runs, just inference and analysis.*

2. **[Addressing "Documentation Debt": A Retrospective Datasheet for BookCorpus](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.05241)** — **2 authors** (Bandy & Vincent, then grad students), NeurIPS Datasets & Benchmarks 2021. Found likely copyright violations, thousands of duplicated books, and genre skew in the corpus behind GPT-1/BERT. Landed because nobody had ever looked; it named "documentation debt" and became a template for retrospective audits. Compute: essentially none.

3. **[A Shocking Amount of the Web is Machine Translated](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.103/)** — **5 authors** (Thompson et al., AWS AI Labs + a UCSB student), ACL Findings 2024. Used multi-way parallelism in CCMatrix (the same sentence appearing translated across many languages, mined from Common Crawl) as a cheap MT fingerprint; showed low-resource-language web text is disproportionately machine-translated junk — a direct data-quality warning for multilingual LLMs. Landed via a clever *indirect* detector (inferring MT from cross-language patterns rather than classifying each text), not an AI-text classifier ([code](https://github.com/amazon-science/multi-way-parallel-ccmatrix/)).

4. **[Proving Test Set Contamination in Black Box Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17623)** — **5 authors** (Oren, Meister, Chatterji, Ladhak, Hashimoto; Stanford/Columbia), **ICLR 2024 oral**. Exchangeability of benchmark orderings (a contaminated model favors the original ordering of a dataset over shuffled versions) gives *provable* p-values for contamination with only log-prob access (the model's reported probability for each token — no weights needed); works on 1.4B models and 1,000-example test sets. Landed because it replaced heuristics with statistical guarantees — a method an evaluation-minded researcher could extend.

5. **[Consent in Crisis: The Rapid Decline of the AI Data Commons](https://www.dataprovenance.org/Consent_in_Crisis.pdf)** — Data Provenance Initiative volunteer collective (lead: Longpre). First longitudinal audit of robots.txt/ToS restrictions across the corpora behind C4/RefinedWeb etc.; ~28% of the most critical C4 sources became fully restricted in about a year ([Open Future summary](https://openfuture.eu/note/consent-in-crisis-the-rapid-decline-of-the-ai-data-commons/)). Landed because it quantified a structural shift everyone suspected; heavily cited in policy discourse. **Venue resolved this pass: NeurIPS 2024 Datasets & Benchmarks Track** ([proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/c3738949a80306cc48a8ea8ba0560f9d-Abstract-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.html)).

6. **[The FineWeb Datasets: Decanting the Web for the Finest Text Data at Scale](https://huggingface.co/papers/2406.17557)** — **8 authors** (Penedo et al., HF), NeurIPS 2024 D&B. 15T tokens plus, crucially, the *published ablation methodology* and the FineWeb-Edu classifier. Landed because it open-sourced the recipe, not just the data, making corpus curation a reproducible science. (Corpus-scale compute — but the paper's classifier/ablation components are small-scale.)

7. **[Is Model Collapse Inevitable?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01413)** — grad-student-led (Gerstgrasser, Schaeffer et al.; Stanford/Harvard/MIT). Rebutted the Nature model-collapse narrative: if synthetic data *accumulates alongside* real data instead of replacing it, test error stays bounded — theory + experiments across LMs, diffusion, VAEs (variational autoencoders, an older class of generative model). Landed by attacking the assumption, not the math, of a high-profile result; reshaped the "web is poisoned" discourse.

8. **[The Rise of AI-Generated Content in Wikipedia](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08044)** — **3 authors** (Brooks, Eggert, Peskoff; Princeton), WikiNLP workshop 2024. >5% of new English Wikipedia articles flagged by two AI-text detectors (GPTZero + open-source Binoculars — tools that guess whether text was machine-written). Landed as the citable number for synthetic-text encroachment on a canonical source; also a case study in the detector-reliability problem it inherits.

*Org-scale contrast:* [Common Pile v0.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05209v1) ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/06/eleutherai-releases-massive-ai-training-dataset-of-licensed-and-open-domain-text/)) took EleutherAI + Toronto/Vector/HF/Ai2 + a dozen institutions **two years** — useful calibration for what does *not* fit a solo program (whole-corpus construction) vs. what does (the license-detection, extraction, and audit components it was assembled from).

## Where the gaps are (named, sourced)

1. **License metadata is broken at the infrastructure level.** The DPI audit found **70%+ license omission and 50%+ license error rates** on major dataset-hosting sites ([Nature MI](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-024-00878-8)). The Mozilla/EleutherAI best-practices paper names the open problems outright: "unreliable metadata and 'data laundering,' locked-in data, digitization costs, jurisdictional uncertainty" ([arXiv 2501.08365](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.08365)). Automated license-verification tooling is nascent (Common Pile had to build its own pipelines — [blog](https://blog.eleuther.ai/common-pile/)).

2. **Consent signaling has no adequate mechanism.** Consent in Crisis documents both the collapse (restrictions rising fast) and the inexpressiveness of robots.txt/ToS for AI-specific preferences; the ICML position paper explicitly frames "missing infrastructure" for authenticity, consent, and provenance ([2404.12691](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12691)). Spawning's registry is voluntary and honored by only some labs ([Spawning](https://spawning.substack.com/p/opt-outs-that-work-in-the-world-of)). Nobody has settled how consent should propagate through derivatives (dataset → filtered subset → synthetic rewrite).

3. **The open-license data pool is too small, and the bottleneck is extraction, not licensing.** Common Pile's own framing: performance trails FineWeb "due to data pool size rather than licensing," with untapped cultural-heritage archives and **no good openly licensed post-training data** ([blog](https://blog.eleuther.ai/common-pile/)). OCR correction for historical text is an active small-model niche (Pleias built dedicated models — [Common Corpus paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01732v1)).

4. **Contamination detection methods rest on shaky assumptions and are easy to evade.** ETH showed evasion is trivial via rephrasing ([2402.02823](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.02823)); a survey directly questions whether current detection assumptions hold ([Does Data Contamination Detection Work (Well)?](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18966v1)). The community's evidence base is tiny: CONDA's database held **566 entries from 23 contributors** ([shared-task report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21530)). Proposed fixes (benchmark watermarking — hiding a detectable marker in test items so leakage can be traced, [2502.17259](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.17259); contamination-resistant benchmark design) are early.

5. **Synthetic-share measurement is methodologically weak.** The widely quoted "50%+ of the web is AI" numbers derive from commercial detectors (SurferSEO via Graphite; see [Futurism](https://futurism.com/artificial-intelligence/over-50-percent-internet-ai-slop)) with known false-positive problems (flagging human text as AI); the Wikipedia study leans on GPTZero/Binoculars ([2410.08044](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08044)). There is no detector-validation-grade estimate (one where the detector's own error rate has been measured and corrected for) of the synthetic fraction of fresh Common Crawl snapshots. ⚠️ inference: this is a measurement-science gap, not a modeling gap — calibration/uncertainty methodology (making the reported number account for detector error) is the missing piece.

6. **Model-collapse empirics under *realistic* web dynamics are unresolved.** The replace-vs-accumulate dichotomy (does synthetic data overwrite real data, or pile up next to it? — [2404.01413](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01413), which *primary-verified this pass* proves test error has a finite upper bound independent of iteration count when data accumulate, across LMs + diffusion + VAEs; vs. the Nature line, which [Borji's KDE note, 2410.12954](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12954) *supports* by arguing collapse "may be unavoidable" as a sampling phenomenon — note this note is a supportive theoretical analysis, **not** a rebuttal) leaves untested the actual regime: accumulation *plus* quality-filtering *plus* selection effects (what humans choose to post and keep skews the mix). Follow-on theory exists ([statistical analysis of collapse](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.05090), [probabilistic perspective](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13947)) but the empirical ecology is open. **This is a genuine open empirical question** — the accumulate+filter+selection regime has not been measured end-to-end, and remains a structural gap.

7. **Tokenizer equity has numbers but few fixes.** Documented 2–15× token-cost premiums against low-resource languages ([tokenization premiums](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.13328), [tokenizer tax](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.24718), [Do All Languages Cost the Same?](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/376393760_Do_All_Languages_Cost_the_Same_Tokenization_in_the_Era_of_Commercial_Language_Models)); post-hoc vocabulary-patching (retrofitting a trained model's tokenizer with better tokens after the fact) is brand new. The field only got a dedicated venue in July 2025 ([TokShop](https://icml.cc/virtual/2025/workshop/39998)) — i.e., it is *just now* organizing, which is when small contributors get outsized visibility.

8. **Single-point-of-failure infrastructure.** Nearly every open corpus flows from Common Crawl, a nonprofit that ran ~15 years on ~$4M from one founder, now taking money from the AI labs whose training sets depend on it ([Dallas Card](https://dallascard.github.io/granular-material/post/financing-common-crawl/), [Mozilla report](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/research/library/generative-ai-training-data/common-crawl/)) — while paywalled sites increasingly block it, biasing what future corpora can contain.
