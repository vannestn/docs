# Evaluation Methodology & the Standards Layer (mid-2026)

*Researched July 2026. Every claim below comes from a page opened during research unless marked "⚠️ unverified" or "(inferred)". Inline links point at the source actually read.*

> **How to read this brief.** This is the "meta" layer of AI evaluation — the study of how to make evals *trustworthy*, and how that trust becomes regulation. Where the text names a paper or a technical term, a plain-language gloss follows in parentheses or an em-dash so you can grasp it without opening the link. The [Where the gaps are](#where-the-gaps-are) section is written as **opportunity deep-dives**: each gap gets context + definitions, concrete methodology options, and an honest feasibility/challenge read.

## The shape of this space — what the work actually consists of

This is the meta-layer of AI evaluation: not building benchmarks, but making evaluation itself trustworthy — and turning that trustworthiness into standards regulators can lean on. Five distinguishable sub-areas:

**1. The "science of evals" agenda (measurement + construct validity).**
The founding text is Apollo Research's [We Need a Science of Evals](https://www.apolloresearch.ai/science/we-need-a-science-of-evals/) (Jan 2024). Its argument: evals drive high-stakes decisions (a lab's Responsible Scaling Policy, a government's risk assessment) yet lack the basics of a science — **replicability** (same test, same result), **statistical guarantees** (error bars, significance), and **coverage** (testing the whole capability, not a convenient slice). Its headline demonstration: "simple formatting changes... can lead to a ~5 percentage point change in accuracy" — i.e., the same model scores differently just because you reworded the prompt template, which means many reported numbers are noise.

A widely-read [response by Hernández-Orallo & Martínez-Plumed](https://aievaluation.substack.com/p/a-response-to-we-need-a-science-of) countered that this "new" agenda re-discovers decades of **psychometrics** (the science of measuring latent human traits like IQ) and **measurement theory**, plus software testing and NIST's long evaluation history — the tools already exist; ML just isn't using them. The academic follow-through built on that tension:

- [Wallach et al., "Evaluating Generative AI Systems Is a Social Science Measurement Challenge"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00561) (ICML 2025, Microsoft Research–led, 20 authors). *What it argues:* borrow the social-sciences ladder for measuring fuzzy concepts — **background concept** (the vague thing you care about, e.g. "reasoning") → **systematized concept** (a precise definition) → **measurement instrument** (the actual test) → **instance measurements** (the scores). ML, they say, "jumps straight from background concepts to measurement instruments" — i.e., builds a benchmark for "reasoning" without ever pinning down what reasoning means, so nobody can say whether the benchmark measures it.
- [Weidinger, Raji et al., "Toward an Evaluation Science for Generative AI Systems"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05336) (Mar 2025; DeepMind/Berkeley/MSR/Stanford). *What it argues:* copy how safety-critical fields (aviation, pharma) evaluate — three lessons: (1) a metric must connect to **real-world performance**, not just leaderboard rank; (2) metrics must be **iteratively refined** as you learn; (3) you need evaluation **institutions and norms** (bodies, review, incentives), not just papers.
- [Bean et al., "Measuring what Matters: Construct Validity in LLM Benchmarks"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04703) (NeurIPS 2025 D&B; Oxford Internet Institute–led). **Construct validity** = does a test actually measure the concept it claims to, versus something incidental (a "math reasoning" test that really measures reading speed has low construct validity). *What it did:* ~29 expert reviewers systematically audited **445 benchmarks** and found most have vague phenomenon definitions and almost never run statistical tests; it ships an operational checklist for builders.
- [BetterBench (Reuel et al., NeurIPS 2024 spotlight, Stanford)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.12990). *What it did:* defined **46 quality criteria** for a benchmark (across design, implementation, documentation, maintenance) and scored **24 popular benchmarks** against them; finding: "most benchmarks do not report statistical significance... nor allow easy replication." Living scoreboard at [betterbench.stanford.edu](https://betterbench.stanford.edu/).

**2. Statistics of evals.**
Anchor: [Miller (Anthropic), "Adding Error Bars to Evals"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640) ([blog](https://www.anthropic.com/research/statistical-approach-to-model-evals)). *What it did:* treats running an eval as running an **experiment**, and hands over the missing statistics — how to compute **standard errors** (the ± on a score), **paired-difference tests** (compare two models on the *same* questions, which cancels question-difficulty noise and detects smaller true gaps), and **power analysis** (how many questions you need to reliably see a real difference). Its most-cited result: **clustered standard errors** — when questions come in groups (five questions about one shared reading passage aren't five independent data points) the honest error bar can be **>3× larger** than the naive one, so many "X beats Y" claims aren't statistically real. Notably a **single-author** paper. The construct-validity and BetterBench reviews above show these methods are still rarely used — and *that adoption gap is itself the research area*.

**3. Contamination detection.**
"Contamination" = the benchmark's questions (or close variants) leaked into the model's training data, so a high score reflects memorization, not capability.
- The [CONDA workshop](https://conda-workshop.github.io/) (ACL 2024–) made this a formal topic; its [shared-task report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21530) built a public, community-maintained evidence database — **566 contamination entries across 91 sources from 23 contributors**, extended via GitHub pull requests (so a solo person can contribute one finding at a time).
- [Cheng, Chang & Wu (Jilin University), "A Survey on Data Contamination for LLMs"](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.14425v1) organizes detection methods by how much model access you have: **white-box** (you have the weights — check n-gram/text overlap, embedding similarity, or internal activations), **gray-box** (you can see token probabilities — spot memorization via **Min-K%**, i.e. the model is suspiciously un-surprised on the least-likely tokens of a test item, or low **perplexity** = unusually confident), and **black-box** (outputs only — probe by paraphrasing multiple-choice options or comparing output distributions). Fixes: keep test sets moving (dynamic/live benchmarks), rewrite items, or prevent leakage (encryption, access control, inference-time filtering).

**4. Saturation analysis.**
**Saturation** = top models all score near the ceiling, so the benchmark can no longer tell them apart (it has lost *discriminative power*). MMLU-class tests are functionally saturated for frontier models (⚠️ specific % claims come from secondary blogs, e.g. [Kili's 2026 guide](https://kili-technology.com/blog/ai-benchmarks-guide-the-top-evaluations-in-2026-and-why-theyre-not-enough)). Two responses: (a) build harder tests — [Humanity's Last Exam](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09962-4) (2,500 expert questions, in Nature, with an [independently verified subset by Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/hle)); and (b) — more this brief's territory — **study saturation itself**: ["When AI Benchmarks Plateau"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16763) (190 benchmarks across 61 lab reports, 2022–2025), the ["Benchmark Health Index"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.11674) (scores 106 benchmarks on discrimination / anti-saturation / impact), and ["The Ouroboros of Benchmarking"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.01365) (nearly all pre-2025 benchmarks already surpassed). This lifecycle/decay analysis is a young, data-analysis-shaped niche.

**5. The standards & regulatory layer.**
- **NIST / CAISI (US).** The former AI Safety Institute was reorganized as the [Center for AI Standards and Innovation](https://www.nist.gov/caisi), "industry's primary point of contact" for testing — it runs unclassified evals (cyber/biosecurity focus), writes guidance, and represents the US in international standards. ⚠️ Secondary sources ([ExecutiveGov](https://www.executivegov.com/articles/nists-caisi-openmined-ai-evaluation-privacy), [Cybersecurity Dive](https://www.cybersecuritydive.com/news/nist-ai-model-testing-caisi-google-microsoft/819452/), [Microsoft](https://blogs.microsoft.com/on-the-issues/2026/05/05/advancing-ai-evaluation-with-the-center-for-ai-standards-us-and-innovation-and-the-ai-security-institute-uk/)) report 40+ evaluations and pre-deployment agreements with five frontier developers by mid-2026 — not confirmed on nist.gov directly. The **ARIA** program ([launch](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2024/05/nist-launches-aria-new-program-advance-sociotechnical-testing-and), [pilot report NIST AI 700-2](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.700-2.pdf)) is NIST's **sociotechnical field-testing** effort — testing AI with real users in realistic settings rather than on fixed question sets. Its pilot: 3 scenarios (TV spoilers, meal planning, travel), 3 layers (fixed prompts → red-teaming → real-world use), 1,500+ annotations by 7 staff over 508 sessions, producing a "Contextual Robustness Index (CoRIx)" — a score for how well performance holds up in messy real conditions. The [International Network for Advanced AI Measurement, Evaluation and Science](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2026/02/international-network-advanced-ai-measurement-evaluation-and-science) (10 members: AU, CA, EU, FR, JP, KE, KR, SG, UK, US) published **consensus areas on automated-evaluation practices** (Feb 2026) — the clearest sign that "eval best practices" are becoming intergovernmental standards material.
- **EU AI Act + GPAI Code of Practice.** The [Code of Practice](https://code-of-practice.ai/) ("GPAI" = general-purpose AI; published 10 July 2025; [Commission page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/contents-code-gpai)) is a *voluntary* tool that, if signed, is the practical way to show compliance. Its Safety & Security chapter (for **systemic-risk** models only — the largest, most capable general models) requires a written framework whose pipeline is: model-independent risk info → **model evaluations** → risk modeling → risk estimation → post-market monitoring; documents kept 10 years; enforcement (Art. 101) begins **2 Aug 2026** ([Latham & Watkins](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/eu-ai-act-gpai-model-obligations-in-force-and-final-gpai-code-of-practice-in-place)). [CSET's analysis](https://cset.georgetown.edu/article/eu-ai-code-safety/): evals must cover four named systemic risks — **CBRN** (chemical/bio/radiological/nuclear uplift), **loss of control**, **cyber offense**, and **harmful manipulation** — via "benchmarks, red teaming, and human uplift studies" (**human uplift study** = measure how much *more* dangerous-capable a person becomes *with* the model vs. without, via a controlled trial). OpenAI/Google/Mistral/Anthropic supported it; Meta refused. Key gap CSET names: acceptable risk thresholds are "almost universally undefined," and fewer than half of reviewed firms substantively test dangerous capabilities.
- **Harmonized standards (the AI Act's technical backbone).** A **harmonized standard** is a technical spec that, once published in the EU Official Journal, gives products a legal "presumption of conformity" — follow it and you're deemed compliant. For high-risk AI these come from [CEN-CENELEC JTC 21](https://jtc21.eu/) and are badly delayed: original deadline Apr 2025, [acceleration package Oct 2025](https://www.cencenelec.eu/news-events/news/2025/brief-news/2025-10-23-ai-standardization/) now targeting Q4 2026 — leaving providers months, not years, to implement them.
- **ISO/IEC.** [ISO/IEC 42001:2023](https://www.iso.org/standard/42001) is an **AI management-system standard** (it governs your *processes* for managing AI risk, like ISO 9001 does for quality — it does *not* tell you which evals to run). Written by [JTC 1/SC 42](https://www.iso.org/obp/ui/en/#!iso:std:81230:en) (50+ AI standards); individual experts join through their national standards body; certification is by accredited third parties, not ISO.
- **MLCommons AILuminate.** [v1.0 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05731) (~102 authors — consortium scale). *What it is:* a standardized **safety** benchmark — 12 hazard categories, five letter-grades (Poor→Excellent) assigned *relative to reference models*, graded by an ensemble of tuned open safety-classifier models. Self-named limits: single-turn only, content hazards only, grader uncertainty. [Current state](https://mlcommons.org/ailuminate/): English/French/Chinese safety suites live, jailbreak + agentic-reliability + multimodal in progress. The [AI Risk & Reliability WG](https://mlcommons.org/working-groups/ai-risk-reliability/ai-risk-reliability/) "welcome[s] others to join" (100+ orgs; ⚠️ unverified whether unaffiliated individuals can join free).

**6. Tooling ecosystems (the de-facto standards).**
An **eval harness** is the software that actually runs a model against a benchmark and scores it — the harness's defaults quietly decide what everyone's numbers mean.
- **[Inspect](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/)** (UK AISI): open-sourced May 2024, created by **JJ Allaire** (RStudio/Posit founder) ([interview](https://parlance-labs.com/education/evals/allaire.html)); [Inspect Evals](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/inspect-evals) is the community benchmark repo (maintained with **Arcadia Impact** + **Vector Institute**, 50+ contributors); [adopted by Apollo](https://www.apolloresearch.ai/blog/apollo-is-adopting-inspect/). The closest thing to a de-facto standard for safety-institute-grade evals; includes a [sandbox toolkit for agent evals](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/the-inspect-sandboxing-toolkit-scalable-and-secure-ai-agent-evaluations).
- **[lm-eval-harness](https://github.com/EleutherAI/lm-evaluation-harness)** (EleutherAI): backbone of the HF Open LLM Leaderboard era; its paper ["Lessons from the Trenches on Reproducible Evaluation"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14782) is the canonical account of *why eval numbers don't reproduce* (prompt formatting, scoring choices, setup drift).
- **[HELM](https://github.com/stanford-crfm/helm)** (Stanford CRFM): "holistic" multi-metric framework with domain leaderboards (MedHELM, VHELM…). Per its README it **entered maintenance mode June 1, 2026** — a live example of academic eval-infrastructure fragility.
- **[OpenAI Evals](https://github.com/openai/evals)**: hosted platform being deprecated (read-only Oct 31 2026, shutdown Nov 30 2026 per [docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/evals)); [simple-evals](https://github.com/openai/simple-evals) frozen since July 2025. ⚠️ platform-vs-repo details partially unverified.
- **Commercial observability/eval layer**: [Langfuse](https://laminar.sh/article/langfuse-alternatives-2026) (OSS, MIT, ~28k stars), LangSmith, [Braintrust](https://www.braintrust.dev/articles), W&B Weave, Arize Phoenix, Confident AI/DeepEval. This is where "evals" meets ordinary product engineering — commercially crowded but methodologically thin (inferred: vendors compete on tracing/UX, not measurement validity). ⚠️ One source ([Firecrawl](https://www.firecrawl.dev/blog/best-llm-observability-tools)) reports OpenAI acquired **promptfoo** (Mar 2026, core staying MIT) — unverified elsewhere.

## Published exemplars

Each of these is here as an existence proof that a small team — often one person — can move this field, and *why* the work landed:

1. **[Adding Error Bars to Evals](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640)** — Evan Miller, **solo**, Anthropic (Nov 2024). Converted a vague complaint ("evals lack rigor") into usable formulas with a headline result (naive error bars understate uncertainty ~3× on clustered data). Proof one statistically-literate person can shift field norms.
2. **[We Need a Science of Evals](https://www.apolloresearch.ai/science/we-need-a-science-of-evals/)** — Apollo (Jan 2024), a *blog post, not a paper*. Named the agenda; still the citation anchor, and triggered a terrain-mapping [rebuttal](https://aievaluation.substack.com/p/a-response-to-we-need-a-science-of).
3. **[BetterBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.12990)** — Reuel + 5, Stanford (NeurIPS 2024 spotlight). Grad-student-led meta-evaluation; landed by making "benchmark quality" measurable and public.
4. **[Measuring what Matters](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04703)** — Oxford, NeurIPS 2025 D&B, PhD-student-led. Quantified the validity crisis over 445 benchmarks + shipped a checklist; got [CACM coverage](https://cacm.acm.org/news/measuring-what-matters-in-large-language-model-performance/).
5. **[Lessons from the Trenches](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14782)** — EleutherAI (Biderman et al.). Documented, from 3 years maintaining lm-eval-harness, exactly where eval numbers diverge — the practitioner ground truth position papers cite.
6. **[CONDA 2024 Shared Task](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21530)** — 23 contributors; a standing public contamination database anyone can extend one PR at a time — the lowest-friction on-ramp in the space.
7. **[Social Science Measurement Challenge](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00561)** — Wallach et al., ICML 2025. Imported a mature discipline's vocabulary and gave the "science of evals" crowd theoretical footing.
8. **[Black-Box Access is Insufficient for Rigorous AI Audits](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.14446)** — Casper et al., FAccT 2024, grad-student-led. Structured the auditor-access debate (black/white/outside-the-box access) now shaping third-party-evaluation policy.

## Where the gaps are

Each gap below is written as an **opportunity deep-dive**: what it is (with definitions), why it's open, concrete methodology options, and an honest feasibility/challenge read for a solo, ≤$500-per-study, API-scale researcher. Ratings: **Fit** (match to the researcher's eval/LLM-as-judge/data-pipeline profile) and **Difficulty** are the compiler's estimates, not measured.

### Gap 1 — Eval statistics exist but nobody uses them
**Context.** Miller's error-bar methods (clustered SEs, paired tests, power analysis) are ~2 years old, yet both big 2024–25 meta-reviews ([construct validity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04703), [BetterBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.12990)) find most benchmarks and leaderboards still report a bare number with no uncertainty. The science is settled; the *practice* hasn't moved. That adoption gap — not new math — is the opportunity.
**Why it's a strong opportunity.** It needs only public leaderboard data + reference implementations; the "so what" is immediate (a widely-cited ranking may be statistical noise); and it sits exactly on the researcher's published strength (agreement statistics, uncertainty).
**Approaches.**
1. *Leaderboard statistical audit.* Take a prominent public leaderboard (e.g. an Arena-style or HF-style board), recompute results with proper clustered SEs and paired tests, and report how many "A > B" gaps evaporate at p<0.05. Ship a short paper + a reusable notebook. *Feasibility:* very high — public data, laptop, days-to-weeks. *Challenges:* raw per-item scores aren't always published (you may need to re-run some evals via API, adding modest cost); leaderboard owners may push back — pre-register your method to stay credible.
2. *Harness pull request.* Add clustered-SE / paired-test output as a default to Inspect or lm-eval-harness. *Feasibility:* high on skill, medium on adoption (maintainers must merge it). *Challenges:* engineering to their standards; the *maintainer relationship* is the real gate — start by contributing small fixes first.
3. *"Stat-check" tool.* A small library/web tool that ingests eval results and returns the correct error bars + a power estimate ("you need N more items to call this"). *Feasibility:* high. *Challenges:* tools need distribution to matter — pair with approach 1 as the case study.
**Fit: very high · Difficulty: low–medium.** Best first-study candidate in this brief (it's a sharper version of solo-agenda item *QuantJudge*).

### Gap 2 — Regulators now require evals that barely exist (manipulation, loss-of-control)
**Context.** The EU Code of Practice mandates evaluation of four systemic risks including **harmful manipulation** (does the model persuade/deceive users against their interest) and **loss of control**; [CSET](https://cset.georgetown.edu/article/eu-ai-code-safety/) reads current practice as far below the bar — thresholds "almost universally undefined," under half of firms substantively testing dangerous capabilities. There is **no AILuminate-style standard benchmark for manipulation/persuasion** (inferred from the CoP requirement vs. the benchmark landscape here). So the law demands a measurement that the field hasn't built.
**Why it's an opportunity (with caveats).** High policy relevance and a genuine vacuum. But "loss of control" and CBRN uplift are info-hazard-laden and need frontier-model access — a poor solo fit. **Manipulation/persuasion is the tractable slice**: it can be studied behaviorally over APIs with consenting human raters or model-based proxies.
**Approaches.**
1. *A persuasion/manipulation micro-benchmark.* Define a narrow, well-scoped construct (e.g. "sycophantic agreement under user pressure" or "steering toward a pre-set conclusion"), build a small dataset of scenarios, and score models with a validated judge. *Feasibility:* medium — API-scale, but construct definition is the hard part (see Gap 1's construct-validity lesson). *Challenges:* manipulation is contested to define; human-subjects persuasion studies need IRB/ethics care; avoid building an actual manipulation tool (dual-use — keep it defensive/measurement-only).
2. *A "readiness gap" analysis.* Systematically map each CoP-required risk to whether a public benchmark exists, its quality (via BetterBench-style criteria), and what's missing — a citable policy artifact. *Feasibility:* high (desk research + your eval taxonomy skills). *Challenges:* it's analysis, not measurement — impact depends on distribution to the policy audience (see brief 07 publishing pathways).
**Fit: medium (manipulation slice) · Difficulty: medium–high.** Cross-reference [problem-04 governance](problem-04-governance-geopolitics.md).

### Gap 3 — Multi-turn and agentic safety evaluation is officially a hole
**Context.** AILuminate's own paper restricts itself to **single-turn, content-only** hazards and names multi-turn / multimodal / multilingual as future work ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05731)); MLCommons' agentic-reliability standard is still a "workstream." "Agentic evaluation" = testing a model that takes actions over many steps (using tools, browsing, running code), where safety failures emerge from the *trajectory*, not one reply.
**Why it's an opportunity.** The whole industry pivoted to agents but the safety-eval layer for them is admittedly immature; even partial, well-measured contributions are citable.
**Approaches.**
1. *Multi-turn extension of an existing single-turn safety suite.* Take a public single-turn hazard set and study how safety degrades over conversation turns (does refusal erode? does a benign multi-turn path reach a harmful end?). *Feasibility:* medium — API-scale; the design (what counts as a turn-level failure) is the work. *Challenges:* labeling multi-turn transcripts is costly — lean on LLM-as-judge (your strength) with a human-validated subset.
2. *Agent-trajectory reliability metrics.* Reproduce a public agent benchmark and measure *consistency/variance* across seeds and phrasings rather than just pass@1 — how flaky are the headline agent numbers? *Feasibility:* medium; cost depends on trajectory length (flag against the ≤$500 ceiling — long agent runs add up). *Challenges:* agent harness setup; cost control; this is the solo-agenda *TrajJudgeBench* idea.
**Fit: high · Difficulty: medium.**

### Gap 4 — Harmonized-standards vacuum under the AI Act
**Context.** The technical standards that make the AI Act operational (see the [harmonized standards](#5-the-standards--regulatory-layer) definition above) slipped from Apr 2025 toward Q4 2026 ([CEN-CENELEC](https://www.cencenelec.eu/news-events/news/2025/brief-news/2025-10-23-ai-standardization/)), leaving providers ~6–8 months to comply once they publish ([analysis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13600834.2025.2570966)). Work that translates eval methodology into standard-ready **test procedures** lands in an unusually empty space right now.
**Why it's a *weak* solo fit (stated honestly).** Standards-writing happens inside committees you join via a national body — slow, credential-gated, consensus-driven; not where a part-time outsider ships a quick artifact. Include it for completeness, not as a near-term pick.
**Approaches (if pursued).** Contribute to a national mirror committee of JTC 21 via your national standards body; or publish a "reference test procedure" for one narrow requirement and try to get it cited. *Feasibility:* low for solo/part-time. *Challenges:* pace, access, and that influence here is institutional, not artifact-driven.
**Fit: low · Difficulty: high (structural, not technical).**

### Gap 5 — Contamination detection for closed models is weak
**Context.** In the [Jilin survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.14425v1)'s taxonomy, **black-box** detection (outputs only — the only option for closed APIs) is the least-developed tier, and *no standard anywhere requires linking contamination evidence to score claims* (inferred — no such requirement in the NIST/EU/MLCommons materials read here). So the models that matter most (closed frontier APIs) are the hardest to check, and there's no norm forcing the check.
**Why it's an opportunity.** Methodologically real, entirely API-scale, and connects to a live policy vacuum (nobody has to disclose contamination). Data-pipeline skills transfer directly.
**Approaches.**
1. *Black-box contamination probes at scale.* Implement and stress-test existing black-box methods (paraphrase-consistency, distributional / "guess the missing option" tests) across current closed models on suspected-contaminated benchmarks; report which methods actually detect known contamination. *Feasibility:* high — API + analysis. *Challenges:* black-box methods have high false-positive rates — you need a clean positive control (a benchmark you *know* leaked) to calibrate.
2. *A contamination-disclosure standard proposal + scorecard.* Define what a "contamination statement" for a benchmark result should contain, then score recent high-profile results against it. *Feasibility:* high. *Challenges:* adoption/distribution.
3. *Contribute to the CONDA evidence DB.* Lowest-friction: add verified contamination findings via PR. *Feasibility:* very high (a weekend). *Challenges:* incremental credibility rather than a headline artifact — good as a starter.
**Fit: high · Difficulty: medium.**

### Gap 6 — Saturation / benchmark-lifecycle science is brand new
**Context.** The systematic saturation studies only appeared in late 2025–2026 ([plateau study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16763), [Benchmark Health Index](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.11674)). Open questions: how to *measure* a benchmark's remaining discriminative power, how fast benchmarks decay, and what objective **retirement criteria** should trigger replacement. This is data-analysis-shaped — exactly XGBoost/classical-ML/pipeline territory.
**Why it's a strong opportunity.** New enough that the basic instruments aren't settled; needs only public score histories; and produces reusable artifacts (a metric, a dashboard) that others adopt — the adoption path to becoming "the saturation person."
**Approaches.**
1. *A live "benchmark health" dashboard.* Continuously compute discrimination/saturation metrics across many benchmarks from public leaderboard histories; publish as an updating site. *Feasibility:* high; pairs perfectly with your radar habit. *Challenges:* assembling clean longitudinal score data; sustaining updates (but that longitudinal upkeep *is* the moat — few will).
2. *Retirement-criteria methodology.* Propose and validate a rule for "this benchmark is saturated, retire it," back-tested against benchmarks that already died. *Feasibility:* high (laptop). *Challenges:* defining ground-truth "death"; getting a proposed standard noticed.
3. *Predictive saturation model.* Fit a model that forecasts when a benchmark will saturate from its early trajectory. *Feasibility:* medium. *Challenges:* limited longitudinal data; overfitting.
**Fit: very high · Difficulty: low–medium.**

### Gap 7 — Real-world / field validity
**Context.** **Field validity** = whether benchmark scores predict how the model performs for real users in real tasks. ARIA's pilot ([NIST AI 700-2](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.700-2.pdf)) is the first government attempt at field-testing with real users; Weidinger et al.'s first lesson (metrics must connect to real-world performance) is still mostly unmet. Almost everything is measured on static question sets, not deployments.
**Why it's an opportunity (with friction).** It's arguably the most *important* gap (it's what makes evals mean anything), but field studies with real users bring recruitment cost and IRB/ethics overhead — the least "solo, ≤$500" of the set.
**Approaches.**
1. *Benchmark-to-reality correlation study.* For a task with both a benchmark and a public real-world signal (e.g. a coding benchmark vs. resolved-issue data), measure how well benchmark rank predicts real outcomes. *Feasibility:* medium — desk-scale if a real-world signal is public. *Challenges:* finding a clean real-world signal; confounds.
2. *Small structured field study.* A modest, consented crowd study comparing model choices' benchmark scores to task success. *Feasibility:* low–medium (recruitment + IRB). *Challenges:* cost, ethics, sample size — the binding constraints are people and process, not compute.
**Fit: medium · Difficulty: medium–high.**

### Gap 8 — Infrastructure fragility & governance of shared eval tooling
**Context.** HELM went to maintenance mode (June 2026, [README](https://github.com/stanford-crfm/helm)); OpenAI's Evals platform is shutting down (Nov 2026). Shared eval infrastructure is consolidating on Inspect + community repos — and *who sustains and governs it* is an unsolved question. This is more a contribution surface than a research topic.
**Approaches.** Become a durable maintainer/contributor to Inspect Evals or lm-eval-harness (credibility through infrastructure); or write the first analysis of eval-infrastructure sustainability (funding models, bus-factor, governance). *Feasibility:* high (contribution) / medium (analysis). *Challenges:* maintenance is a slow-burn reputation play, not a quick artifact.
**Fit: medium · Difficulty: low (to start contributing).**

### Gap 9 — No metadata standard / results are non-comparable
**Context.** EvalEval's [Every Eval Ever](https://evalevalai.com/events/shared-task-every-eval-ever/) and [Evaluation Cards](https://huggingface.co/blog/evaleval/evaluation-cards-launch) exist precisely because eval results are scattered, undocumented, and not comparable across papers. A **metadata standard** = an agreed schema for describing an eval (what it tests, how, on what, with what settings) so results can be aggregated.
**Why it's an opportunity.** Explicitly open, with an ACL 2026 shared task offering a co-authorship path — the single clearest "join and contribute" on-ramp.
**Approaches.** Join the EvalEval shared task / working group and contribute schema + tooling; or build an aggregator that ingests eval results into the schema and exposes cross-benchmark queries. *Feasibility:* high (community-backed). *Challenges:* consensus schema work is slow; standards adoption is the perennial risk.
**Fit: high · Difficulty: low–medium.**

> **Opportunity summary.** Strongest solo fits, roughly ranked: **Gap 1** (stat audits) and **Gap 6** (saturation science) — both low-cost, high-fit, artifact-producing — then **Gap 5** (black-box contamination) and **Gap 9** (metadata, community-backed), then **Gap 3** (agentic safety). Gaps 2 and 7 are higher-impact but higher-friction; Gaps 4 and 8 are institutional/contribution plays, not quick studies. All feasibility/fit labels are estimates to be pressure-tested in the [Stage-1 reading + pilot process](../04-topic-selection-process.md), not conclusions.
